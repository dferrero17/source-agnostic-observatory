\title{Operating LLM-Generated Data Wrappers in Production:\\
A 149-Day Field Study of What Breaks in a Smart-City Intake Layer}

% Anonymized for ICTAI double-blind review. Fill in for camera-ready.
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Author(s)}}

\begin{abstract}
\sys{} is a smart city observatory whose intake layer is built from
LLM-generated data wrappers: a non-technical user registers a source and its
metadata through a form, and the platform writes, runs, and maintains the
extraction code itself for sources in three families (REST APIs, spreadsheets,
CSV files), each mapped to a normalized $(\textrm{timestamp}, \textrm{value})$
series. We report 149 days of operating this in production, \nWrappers{} wrappers
and \nDataPoints{} measurements, and three findings that hold across the run.
First, the binding failures are not bad generated code but the environment around
it: 54 of the \nErrorW{} terminal errors are idle pollers killed by a watchdog set
shorter than their cadence and 14 are model-provider faults (quota, rate limit,
overload, or a service restart mid-generation), while
generated code that the repair loop could not fix numbers exactly one (itself
quota-blocked). Second, cheap generation decouples attempt from yield: only
\nNonzeroPointWrappers{} of \nWrappers{} wrappers ever produced data, so attempting
a feed is nearly free but a live indicator is not. Third, crash-triggered LLM
repair leans toward masking rather than fixing: three of the four case studies
recover by swallowing errors, and we down-scope ``recovery'' to restored execution
because the snapshot cannot confirm the values are right. The autonomic MAPE-K
loop and its LLM actuator are an organizing lens, not a contribution; the repair
mechanism is established LLM program repair. What is new is the production evidence
and what it says about where such a system actually breaks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Running a city on evidence means tracking many numbers at once, and those numbers
neither sit in one place nor arrive in one form. Air and water quality come off
environmental sensors and monitoring APIs; overnight stays and lodging capacity
come from tourism boards; energy use, mobility counts, and cultural attendance
come from utilities, transport operators, and municipal departments. The
publishers differ and so do the ways in to their data, a REST endpoint here, an
Excel workbook or a CSV export there, along with the field names, the way dates
are written, and the language they are written in. None of it stands still,
either: an indicator earns its place only while it stays current, so every feed
has to be pulled again and again as new readings land, and every upstream change
of format or endpoint has to be taken in without breaking the series a city has
already built up. What an observatory confronts is therefore a standing
integration problem over many heterogeneous sources, not a task that is finished
once.

The usual way to take a source in is to write an extraction wrapper for it and
then keep that wrapper alive as the source shifts underneath. This cost, paid
once per source to build and then indefinitely to maintain, is why mediator-style
integration has never reached past a small set of
feeds~\cite{wiederhold1992mediators,kushmerick2000wrapper,lerman2003wrapper}, and
it sets the ceiling on coverage: an observatory ends up showing whichever feeds
someone found time to wire and to nurse, rather than the city.

We call an intake layer \emph{format-adaptive} when onboarding a feed costs no
per-source extraction code: a domain user registers the source and its metadata
through a form, and the platform writes, runs, and maintains the wrapper from
there. The adaptivity is bounded, and we are explicit about it: the platform
adapts to a source's format and transport within the structured tabular family,
REST/JSON APIs, spreadsheets, and delimited files, all projected onto one
univariate $(\textrm{timestamp}, \textrm{value})$ series. It is not adaptivity to
data modality or schema. This paper reports \sys{} (anonymized for review), a
smart city observatory running for a European region, where format-adaptive
intake is obtained by making each wrapper an \emph{autonomic} element in the
sense of Kephart and Chess~\cite{kephart2003vision}: a component that manages
itself through a closed loop over its own lifecycle. The actuator here is a large
language model (LLM) rather than a parameter tuner: it writes the component's code
and rewrites it after a failure. We use this only to describe the setting; the
loop itself is established LLM program repair, not our contribution
(Section~\ref{sec:related}).

We organize the wrapper's three self-* behaviours as the
monitor--analyze--plan--execute loop over shared knowledge (MAPE-K) of the
autonomic-computing blueprint~\cite{ibm2005blueprint,huebscher2008autonomic}.
At registration, \emph{self-generation} (Section~\ref{sec:gen}) plans the
artifact: the LLM completes a fixed template from the indicator's metadata and a
data sample, interrogating a live API through a controlled fetch tool before it
writes anything. \emph{Self-deployment} (Section~\ref{sec:deploy}) is the execute
step that runs unattended: each wrapper is an isolated process the platform
starts, watches, check-points, and resumes from per-wrapper watermarks after any
restart. \emph{Self-healing} (Section~\ref{sec:heal}) is the loop closing on a
live artifact: a crash is detected, the termination is classified, the model
re-plans from the captured trace, and the corrected wrapper goes back out.

We do not claim the repair loop itself as novel; it restates LLM program repair
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). The autonomic monitor--analyze--plan--execute--knowledge
(MAPE-K) loop is a \emph{lens} we use to organize the system and, in particular, to
name the boundary of what the loop can fix. The contributions are the architecture
and its one narrow contract (Section~\ref{sec:arch}) and, mainly, three empirical
findings from the \deploymentDays{}-day run. First, the \emph{binding failures are
environmental, not bad code}: of the \nErrorW{} terminal errors, 54 are idle
pollers killed by a watchdog tighter than their cadence and 14 are model-provider
faults (quota, rate limit, overload, or a mid-generation service restart), while
generated code the loop could not repair numbers exactly
one (Section~\ref{sec:res-faults}). Second, \emph{cheap generation decouples
attempt from yield}: only \nNonzeroPointWrappers{} of \nWrappers{} wrappers ever
produced data (Section~\ref{sec:res-gen}). Third, \emph{crash-triggered repair
leans toward masking}: most case repairs recover by swallowing errors, so restored
execution is not validated data (Sections~\ref{sec:res-heal}
and~\ref{sec:res-cases}). We treat the in/out fault partition as a useful framing
of the first finding, not as a discovery in itself, since the partition is drawn
by definition.\coderepo

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Smart city and tourism observatories}
The smart-tourism literature has long argued for destination-scale data
infrastructure~\cite{gretzel2015smart,buhalis2013smart} and has codified the
indicators worth tracking through schemes such as ETIS~\cite{ec2016etis}, while
field reports describe patchy uptake~\cite{modica2018assessment} and dashboards
assembled mostly from warehouses and surveys~\cite{fuchs2014big}. A recurring
finding in evaluations of smart-destination initiatives is that the binding
constraint on sustained monitoring is getting the data in at
all~\cite{ivarsbaidal2021measuring,ivarsbaidal2023sustainable}. These accounts
say little about how intake is actually built. Our claim is that the cost
of taking in and maintaining a feed, more than the analytics on top, is what
bounds how many indicators an observatory can sustain, and we report an intake
layer that drives that cost down, from production. Cheap intake widens what an
observatory can attempt; not every attempt yields a live indicator, as our yield
numbers later show. The deployment happens to be a tourism observatory, but nothing in the
design ties it to tourism: it needs only structured tabular sources that map onto
a shared time-series target.

\subsection{Autonomic computing and self-* systems}
Autonomic computing proposed self-managing components with closed control loops
as the response to operational complexity~\cite{kephart2003vision}, structured
as a monitor--analyze--plan--execute cycle around a shared knowledge base
(MAPE-K)~\cite{ibm2005blueprint}, and later surveys catalogue its self-* property
families~\cite{huebscher2008autonomic}. \sys{} applies that blueprint to data
wrappers, but swaps the actuator: instead of tuning a fixed component's
parameters, the actuator is an LLM that regenerates the component's source code.
We use this correspondence to organize the exposition, not as a novelty claim.

\subsection{LLMs for code generation, repair, and extraction}
Language models now write runnable programs from prose~\cite{chen2021evaluating},
and automated repair has moved toward LLMs as well~\cite{xia2023automated}.
Several systems close a feedback loop around the generated code, whether through
self-critique~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}, episodic
reflection~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}, or conditioning on execution
results~\cite{chen2024selfdebug}, though the benefit of self-repair is
inconsistent~\cite{olausson2024selfrepair}. Repair has also run in production:
SapFix and Getafix ship automated fixes for crashes and bugs at industrial
scale~\cite{marginean2019sapfix,bader2019getafix}, and agentic LLM repair drives
the generate--run--observe--revise loop autonomously~\cite{bouzenia2024repairagent}.
On the data side, Evaporate established that synthesizing extraction code is
cheaper at scale than calling a model per record~\cite{arora2023evaporate},
AutoScraper builds reusable scrapers with an
agent~\cite{huang2024autoscraper}, and both prompted~\cite{narayan2022wrangle} and
fine-tuned~\cite{zhang2024jellyfish} models handle textbook preprocessing steps.
\textbf{We make no novelty claim for the loop itself}: our repair mechanism is
exactly this established LLM program repair, our generation is Evaporate-style code
synthesis, and our endpoint probing is ordinary tool use. The closest mechanism is
Evaporate (synthesized extractors) and the closest loop is Self-Debug and the
production/agentic repair above. What those systems do not provide, and what we
contribute, is a measured population of \emph{field} faults from which we read the
boundary of what the loop can and cannot fix. Our setting also differs in
incidental ways, the repair trigger is a real production crash arriving weeks
after generation, the unit of repair is the whole artifact, and a fixed univariate
contract bounds what synthesis must achieve, but we treat these as descriptions of
the deployment, not as advances over the systems above.

\subsection{Wrapper induction, maintenance, and ETL automation}
Early wrapper induction derived extraction rules from labelled
examples~\cite{kushmerick2000wrapper}, later systems learned them without
supervision from structural regularities~\cite{crescenzi2001roadrunner}, and the
tooling has been surveyed at length~\cite{laender2002brief,ferrara2014web}.
Keeping a wrapper working as its source drifts, by spotting the break and
re-deriving the rules, was identified as a distinct problem from the
start~\cite{kushmerick2000verification,lerman2003wrapper}. ETL pipelines,
meanwhile, have mostly been designed by hand rather than
generated~\cite{vassiliadis2002conceptual,vassiliadis2009survey}, with automation
reaching adjacent pieces such as schema matching~\cite{rahm2001survey},
large-scale curation~\cite{stonebraker2013tamer}, and
wrangling~\cite{furche2016wrangling}. \sys{} folds both the initial derivation and
the later re-derivation into LLM code generation, which reaches past
template-structured pages to the REST APIs, spreadsheets, and delimited files an
observatory ingests, and it targets exactly the slice an indicator platform needs:
pulling one series from one source into one normalized schema. A separate line of
integration research makes data source-agnostic for the \emph{consumer} by
deferring or learning structure, in pay-as-you-go
dataspaces~\cite{franklin2005dataspaces}, polystores over diverse
engines~\cite{duggan2015bigdawg}, and schema-on-read data lakes; we work the
orthogonal, producer side, building and repairing each source's wrapper through an
autonomic LLM loop. The heterogeneity we span is one of format and transport; the
target stays a single univariate series.

\section{Architecture and the Fixed Time-Series Contract}
\label{sec:arch}

\Sys{} runs as a set of containerized services, user, knowledge-base,
data-collector, resource, and indicator (all Python and FastAPI), reached through
an nginx gateway and fronted by a React dashboard. Control traffic and data
traffic ride separate RabbitMQ brokers, and the resource and indicator services
each keep their own datastore, with an optional Keycloak provider for single
sign-on. Figure~\ref{fig:sysarch} lays out these components and the two planes
that join them: a REST serving plane through the gateway, and an asynchronous
ingestion plane across the brokers along which the wrapper processes emit their
points.

Format-adaptive intake rests on one deliberately thin interface. Whatever its
format, a wrapper emits point batches $\{(x,y)\}$ in which $x$ is an ISO~8601
timestamp and $y$ a floating-point value; the code above that interface is
generated per source, and everything below it is shared, fixed plumbing. The thin
contract is also the boundary: a source is in scope precisely when it can be
projected onto a single $(\textrm{timestamp}, \textrm{value})$ series. Out of
scope by construction are unstructured or scanned content (PDFs, images, free
text), push and streaming transports (Kafka, MQTT, websockets), multivariate,
high-frequency, geospatial, and graph data, and anything that needs a join or
entity resolution across feeds; the unhealable single-column CSV of
Section~\ref{sec:res-cases} is this boundary made concrete. A user registers a
source through a web wizard (a file upload, or an API endpoint and its
credentials) with the indicator's metadata; the resource service then owns that
wrapper for life. On the data side, the collector validates each batch against the
contract and forwards it through the brokers, where the resource service maps
wrapper to resource and the indicator service stores, merges, and serves the
series. Source type is one argument to a single generation routine, not a code
path of its own, so the same routine handles all three formats.

\section{Self-* Wrappers as an Autonomic Manager}
\label{sec:selfstar}

Each wrapper is the element a single autonomic manager looks after. That element
is the generated artifact plus the state that travels with it: the wrapper's
code, its execution watermarks, its retry counter, and its status. The platform
turns the MAPE-K loop~\cite{ibm2005blueprint} on that element, watching process
state, classifying how a process ended, planning a fresh or corrected artifact,
and carrying the plan out by deploying it, all against a shared store of
knowledge. We use this loop only as an organizing lens; its value here is that it
names \emph{which faults the manager has authority over} (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).
The three self-* loops in Figure~\ref{fig:selfstar} are what it yields. In every one of them
the actuator is the same pairing: an LLM that produces and revises code, and a
process supervisor that puts it into service.

actuator is an LLM that writes and revises code together with a supervisor that

\subsection{Shared knowledge}
\label{sec:knowledge}
What binds the loops into one manager, rather than three disconnected
mechanisms, is the knowledge they share. Some of it is fixed. The wrapper
template encodes the Section~\ref{sec:arch} contract as code, and a standing list
of fourteen hard rules captures lessons from earlier failures, among them: leave
the historical-backfill driver alone, invent no data, treat date ranges as
inclusive, keep timezone handling uniform, and read non-English dates such as
localized month names. Together these constrain what the planner is allowed to
emit. The rest is per-wrapper and changes at runtime: a pair of watermarks (the
earliest and latest timestamps sent) with a phase, a retry counter capped at
three, a timestamped execution log, and the stored wrapper document carrying its
metadata, source configuration, current code, and status. The generation loop
writes the first version of the code, the deployment loop reads and advances the
watermarks, and the healing loop reads the code and the log and writes a
successor. The rule list is the part that grows: each diagnosed failure can add a
constraint that every later plan must respect.

\subsection{Self-generation: planning a new artifact}
\label{sec:gen}
The actuator is Google's Gemini Flash, reached through the \texttt{google-genai}
SDK and pinned to the floating \texttt{gemini-flash-latest} alias. It does not
write code freely; it works inside a fixed wrapper class (Figure~\ref{fig:template}). Only a few methods are open to it, extraction
(\texttt{fetch\_external\_data}) and, for APIs, a windowed fetch and a polling
period, while the backfill driver, the message transport, the batching, and the
run loop are library code the prompt tells it not to touch
(Figure~\ref{fig:template}). Jinja2 assembles the prompt. Into it go the
indicator metadata, the source configuration, and a brief sample of the data:
the opening 20 lines of a CSV, 15 rows from each workbook sheet alongside the
sheet names, or a captured API reply. Alongside the sample sit the required
\texttt{\{x,y\}} output shape, the template, instructions specific to the source
type, and the rule list. When the source is an API, the same call hands the model a sandboxed
\texttt{fetch\_url} tool that hands back the first 2500 characters of a live
reply; it may call the tool as many as fifteen times to learn the response's
nesting, its field names, and which date filters the endpoint honours, rather than inferring
them from the URL. Request authentication is built into the headers by the
service from the declared scheme, so the model never handles it. A static check
gates deployment, parsing the file and looking for placeholders left unfilled;
a first failure earns one corrective prompt, a second sends the wrapper to
\texttt{error}. Nothing is executed as a test beforehand, so any runtime fault is
the healing loop's problem.

\subsection{Self-deployment: execute without an operator}
\label{sec:deploy}
Deployment is ordinary process supervision, and we claim nothing new for it; we
record it only because it is what lets the other two loops run unattended. The
resource service runs each wrapper as its own operating-system process under a
concurrency semaphore (default 100) and a per-run watchdog (default 3600\,s). A
file wrapper runs once; an API wrapper back-fills history in size-adapting batches
and then polls at the period its periodicity implies, with no external scheduler.
A monitor reads process state every 30\,s. Each wrapper keeps a low and a high
watermark (its earliest and latest emitted timestamps); after a restart it resumes
from the high mark rather than re-fetching, so a relaunch neither duplicates nor
skips points, the same check-point-and-resume idiom as streaming consumers (Kafka
offsets, Flink checkpoints). Generation produces a process to oversee; healing
produces a replacement to relaunch and resume.

\subsection{Self-healing: the full loop on a live artifact}
\label{sec:heal}
Healing is where every MAPE-K stage shows up at once. The 30\,s monitor reads
each process's exit status (\emph{monitor}). An abnormal exit, in practice always
code~1, is separated from an idle watchdog kill and from a provider error during
generation, and only the first becomes a repair (\emph{analyze}). The plan is an
LLM call carrying the full crashed source, its captured stderr, and the final
twenty lines of stdout, with the instruction to return a complete corrected file
that preserves the class and addresses that particular traceback (\emph{plan});
for an API wrapper it may re-interrogate the endpoint through the same
\texttt{fetch\_url} tool, up to ten times. The returned file goes through the same
static check as a first generation, replaces the old file, bumps the retry
counter, and the process is relaunched and resumed from its watermarks
(\emph{execute}). The manager grants at most three repairs to any wrapper and
leaves the fourth-time loser in \texttt{error} for a person. Two things come from
treating the artifact, not a diff, as the unit of repair. The trigger is a live
exit code that may arrive weeks after generation, so the feedback carries the
source's real behaviour rather than a test author's guess; and because the plan
re-emits the whole wrapper, a repair can re-shape the extraction end to end
instead of touching one
statement~\cite{chen2024selfdebug,olausson2024selfrepair}.

\subsection{Scope of control}
\label{sec:scope}
Format-adaptivity then comes out of the manager rather than from any separate
component. One knowledge base and one set of loops cover API, spreadsheet, and
CSV feeds because source type is a parameter the template takes, so a feed in one
of those families is taken in simply by generating, deploying, and repairing its
wrapper with no per-source code. The same view marks the limit of the manager's
reach. We draw the boundary operationally, before looking at the counts: a fault
is \emph{inside} the manager when a change to the wrapper's code can fix it
(broken extraction logic, a source's formatting quirks), and \emph{outside} when
the fix lies in the environment the code cannot rewrite (a spent model quota, a
stale credential, a watchdog set shorter than a source's polling period).
Section~\ref{sec:res-faults} then asks how the production faults fall relative to
that line. The boundary is a design choice, not a law: the watchdog and polling
period are themselves values the generator sets, so a poll-interval mismatch is
environmental only because we left interval-setting outside the loop, and it could
be brought inside.

\subsection{Self-healing effectiveness}
\label{sec:res-heal}
\nRetried{} wrappers, \pctRetried\% of the total and split 26 spreadsheet, 14
API, and 3 CSV, threw an uncaught exception while running and were taken up by the
repair loop, which shipped \nHealEvents{} redeployments; counted per wrapper, the
repair depth was \retryOnce{} at a single attempt, \retryTwice{} at two, and
\retryThrice{} at three. ``Recovery'' can be read three ways, and they do not
agree, so we keep them apart. Measured by recurrence, the same crash came back
after the last repair for exactly one of the 43, so 42 stayed crash-free.
Measured by final status, \nRecovered{} of 43 (\pctRecovered\%) closed in a
non-error state. Measured by output, 32 resumed sending data after their last
repair, 20{,}351 points, about an eighth of everything collected. The one wrapper
that burned all three attempts never stabilised; it shows a non-error status only
because its resource was deleted afterwards, and we do not score the deepest-retry
case as a win. The repairs themselves were fast and untouched by a person: a
median \healLatencyMedian{}\,s from crash to redeployment (quartiles 24.2 and
50.8\,s, 90th percentile \healLatencyPninety{}\,s, longest \healLatencyMax{}\,s
over the \nHealEvents{} events), up to 30\,s of which is the detection cycle. The
typical crash arrived 16.8\,s into a run, in the fetch-and-parse stage, and none
occurred while a poller waited idle.

The run also offers a pre-automation reference period. For the first 65 days no
repair loop was active (it first appears in the logs on 2026-03-19), and over
that stretch 36 crashes of this same exit class were simply left to die; we read
the comparison descriptively, since the early period differs in source mix and
load and is not a matched control. Two features of the timeline qualify the
healing counts. Thirty-one of the 50 repair events land on a single day, when a
credential fix set off a redeploy that restarted every wrapper together, so the
events are correlated rather than 50 independent failures. The latency is robust
to this. The headline 33.4\,s is over all 50 repair events; restricted to each
wrapper's first repair, the 17 that first crashed on other days have a median
34.0\,s (range 15.5--80.4) against 33.2\,s for the 26-wrapper batch, so the figure
is not an artifact of the mass relaunch. The recovery \emph{counts}, however, do lean on the batch and we
report them with that caveat. And since each relaunch overwrites the stderr file, a
triggering traceback survives only where the last run also crashed; for the
wrappers that recovered we infer the trigger from the fixes their code carries,
which means these counts describe the \emph{repairs} the model made rather than
independently observed causes. So read, a comment or guard pointing at a mended
defect appears in 7 of the 43 crash wrappers but in zero of the 353 clean ones,
with timezone fixes splitting 4-of-43 versus 1-of-353. The next subsection turns
from these repaired crashes to the full population of terminal errors, where the
more consequential result lies.

\subsection{Faults inside and outside the manager}
\label{sec:res-faults}
\input{tab_errors}
The result in this section is the paper's central one: the binding faults are
environmental, not bad code. Table~\ref{tab:errors} sorts the \nErrorW{}
wrappers that ended in \texttt{error} by cause, and few of them indict the
generated code. Fourteen never reached the running stage: nine ran into the
model's daily free-tier quota, one a per-request rate limit, one a provider
overload, and three more were cut off mid-generation when a service restarted.
Another, larger set of 54 are API pollers the 3600\,s watchdog stopped between
polling cycles, 53 of them leaving no traceback; here the watchdog is just shorter
than the source's daily or monthly rhythm, a setting mismatched to the feed rather
than broken code, and 48 of these had already delivered 52{,}372 points before
being cut short. One wrapper crashed and could not be mended because the repair
call itself struck the quota ceiling, and that is the lone error-status case where
real generated code went unrepaired. The lesson is about the workload, not just
the loop: in a production indicator platform the failures that bind are
environmental, not defects in the generated extraction code. The split also makes
the scope of control of Section~\ref{sec:scope} concrete: code defects and
formatting quirks come off the operator's plate, while spent quota, bad
credentials, and limits set for the wrong cadence stay outside the managed element
and stay manual. Those environmental faults are the residual per-source human
surface: the polling period and watchdog follow the declared periodicity, the
quota the chosen model plan, the credentials the registration form. The manager
removes the per-source \emph{code}, not every per-source decision.

\subsection{Repair case studies}
\label{sec:res-cases}
Four wrappers put faces on that partition; the causes in the first two are read
off the fix, the third has its exception quoted in the repaired file, and the
fourth kept its trace because it never recovered. They also expose the masking
risk: three of the four ``heal'' by absorbing an error rather than extracting the
intended data.

\subsubsection{Aware-versus-naive datetime comparison (API, healed)}
A yearly urban-waste API wrapper fell over 82\,s into its backfill while matching
timezone-aware dates from the source against timezone-naive window bounds. Its
repair brings both bounds to naive form before the comparison and downgrades an
empty fetch from an exception to a log line. Back in service after 34.7\,s, it
ran to completion with 596 points.

\subsubsection{Doubled date parameters (API, healed)}
An air-quality wrapper had been given an endpoint URL that already pinned a date
window; the generated code added a second one, and the server replied HTTP~400.
The repair removes every variant of the start and end parameters from the
supplied URL before applying its own window and swallows any leftover 400 rather
than failing. Back after 51.3\,s, it was still streaming at the snapshot, 395
points in.

\subsubsection{Header text in the data rows (spreadsheet, healed)}
A cultural-heritage workbook repeated its localized year header (\texttt{"Ano"})
down among the values, so the wrapper died on \texttt{int("Ano")}; the repaired
file quotes the exception, so the cause is recorded in the fix. The repair swaps
raw integer casts for coercing conversions and discards non-numeric rows, and the
same change turns up in three neighbouring wrappers mended the same night. Back
after 61.6\,s, it finished.

\subsubsection{A missing second column (CSV, not healed)}
The canonical out-of-scope case: a search-interest export came through as one
column, leaving no $(\textrm{time},\textrm{value})$ pair to find. Three repairs
(27.0, 33.0, and 51.8\,s) tried delimiter sniffing and bad-line skipping, but none
could conjure a second column, and the loop stopped. A sibling wrapper reading a
two-column variant of the very same export healed on its first try. The manager
mends code; it cannot supply data the source withholds.

\subsection{Pipeline integrity and throughput}
\label{sec:res-pipeline}
Point counts thin out down the pipeline: of the \nDataPoints{} points the wrappers
emit, 148{,}131 reach the resource store as raw segments and 106{,}442 survive
consolidation there, 100{,}550 land in the indicator store, and chronological
merging leaves series of 84{,}431. The losses trace
to deleted resources (198 of \nWrappers{}, 126 of which left their wrapper
\texttt{stopped}), to regeneration wiping earlier data, to points landing for
resources with no indicator attached, and to timestamp-level deduplication at
merge time. Where we could check integrity, it held: across 30 days the collector
rejected nothing on schema grounds and raised only 18 duplicate-timestamp
warnings, each settled by averaging; over an 8.3-hour control-plane window the
resource service emitted 153 messages and the indicator service took in exactly
153, none lost in the broker; and the gateway handled 55{,}086 requests in about
20 days (near 2{,}750 a day, 80.3\% of them HTTP~200). Resumption proved itself in
the field: a container rebuild a few hours before the snapshot sent all 16
running wrappers through a restart, 15 of them continuing from stored watermarks
while the one missing a high watermark re-read its history from scratch.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Seen as an autonomic manager, the deployment explains both its successes and its
ceiling. Source-specific code stays out of human hands because generation is
fenced in by a fixed contract, because the plan is grounded in what the source
actually returns instead of what it is assumed to return, and because repair
fires on a genuine production trace. The ceiling is precisely the faults that sit
outside the managed element: the single error tied to unrepaired code was waiting
on a quota, and the single crash the loop could not mend was over data that was
not there.

The evidence has limits worth stating plainly. It is observational, drawn from
one deployment, one model family, and one region, with no side-by-side control;
the pre-automation period is a reference rather than a matched arm, and the crash
events are correlated, not independent. We have no hand-written wrappers for the
same sources to measure against, because none were ever built. We score a wrapper as
recovered when it returns to a non-error state, which (as the deepest-retry case
shows) need not mean it ran cleanly and says nothing about whether the values it
extracts are right, since the collector inspects each batch's shape and never the
underlying truth. We could not close this gap from the snapshot: it retains no
copy of the original sources, and the downstream stores are confounded by resource
deletion, regeneration, and merge-time deduplication, so an extracted value cannot
be checked against ground truth. A best-effort look at the healed wrappers' final
stored series is consistent with this caution rather than reassuring against it,
many are sparse or single-valued, and three of the four case-study repairs work by
\emph{swallowing} errors (downgrading an empty fetch to a log line, absorbing a
residual HTTP~400, dropping non-numeric rows), which is exactly the move that can
mask data loss behind a clean exit. We therefore down-scope ``self-healing'' to
restored \emph{execution} and treat value-correctness as open. Relatedly, for the
healed wrappers the cause is read from the repair because the relaunch destroys the
trace, so the fault analysis characterizes the model's fixes more than
independently observed failures. The floating \texttt{gemini-flash-latest} alias pins the model, so its underlying
weights may have shifted across the run; the generation latency
includes queueing and so overstates model time; the memory figure is an idle
reading; and watermark coverage spans are computable for only the 11 wrappers
whose low mark was kept. Two systemic risks deserve their own mention and remain
open: the model quota is a single point of failure already behind 10 of the 69
terminal errors, and running model-written code as supervised production
processes is contained here only by the semaphore and watchdog, not by any
stronger sandbox. Whether the pattern carries beyond 396 wrappers and one region
is argued from its design, not demonstrated.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We operated LLM-generated extraction wrappers as live infrastructure for
\deploymentDays{} days: \nWrappers{} wrappers, \nDataPoints{} points, and of the
\nRetried{} that crashed, all but one stayed crash-free after at most three
unattended repairs (a stricter read by final status leaves \nRecovered{} of 43
non-error and 32 still delivering data), at a median \healLatencyMedian{}\,s that
holds with the one correlated mass-relaunch set aside. The result we stand
behind is empirical: in production the failures that bind are environmental, not
defects in the generated code, and the repair loop governs exactly the code the
LLM owns. The in/out partition is the framing that organizes that finding, not a
discovery in itself. What the loop could not absorb was environmental, which sets
the agenda: checking
extracted values for meaning, and folding quota and credential faults into the
manager as state it owns rather than leaving them on an operator's desk.
