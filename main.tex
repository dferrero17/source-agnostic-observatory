\begin{abstract}
A smart city observatory is only as useful as the breadth of sources it can
ingest, yet most indicator platforms hard-wire a handful of feeds and pay an
engineer to maintain a wrapper for each. We present \sys{}, a production smart
city observatory whose ingestion layer is source-agnostic: any REST API,
spreadsheet, or CSV export becomes a normalized indicator time series with no
source-specific code written by a human. The platform treats each source's data
wrapper as the managed element of an autonomic manager and runs three control
loops over it. Self-generation plans a new artifact: a large language model
fills a fixed extraction template from source metadata and a data sample,
probing live endpoints before it commits to extraction logic. Self-deployment
executes it: wrappers run as isolated, supervised, checkpointed processes that
the platform launches and relaunches on its own. Self-healing closes the loop:
a monitor re-invokes the model with the production crash trace and redeploys a
repaired wrapper. We cast the three loops in MAPE-K terms over one shared
knowledge base and report a \deploymentDays{}-day deployment: \nWrappers{}
wrappers across API, XLSX, and CSV sources ingested \nDataPoints{} data points;
\nRetried{} (\pctRetried\%) crashed and were repaired automatically, with the
crash recurring for only one of them thereafter, at a median of
\healLatencyMedian{}\,s per repair; and the platform runs in about 1.5\,GiB of
memory. We read the result as a scope of control: source heterogeneity and code
defects fall inside the manager's authority, while quota, credential, and
misconfiguration faults are environmental and fall outside it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Smart city and destination observatories promise a single pane of glass over
heterogeneous urban indicators: air and water quality, mobility, energy,
overnight stays, cultural participation. The promise is bounded in practice by
the ingestion layer. Each indicator is published by a different statistical
office, municipality, or sector association, as a REST API, an Excel workbook,
or an ad hoc CSV export, with its own schema, date format, language, and failure
modes. The classical answer is one extraction wrapper per source, but writing
and maintaining those wrappers is the cost that has limited mediator-based data
integration since the
1990s~\cite{wiederhold1992mediators,kushmerick2000wrapper,lerman2003wrapper}.
A platform that can only afford a fixed set of wrappers is, in effect, not an
observatory of its city but of the few sources someone had time to integrate.

We call an ingestion layer \emph{source-agnostic} when adding a new source costs
no source-specific engineering: a domain user registers the source through a web
form, and the platform produces, runs, and maintains the wrapper on its own.
This paper describes \sys{} (anonymized for review), a production smart city
observatory for a European region in which source-agnostic ingestion is
realized by treating each wrapper as an \emph{autonomic} element in the sense of
Kephart and Chess~\cite{kephart2003vision}: a self-managing component with a
closed control loop over its own lifecycle. The actuator that closes the loop is
unusual. Rather than tuning the parameters of a fixed component, a large
language model (LLM) writes the component's code, and rewrites it when it breaks.

Every wrapper has three self-* properties, which we cast as the
monitor--analyze--plan--execute loop over shared knowledge (MAPE-K) of the
autonomic-computing blueprint~\cite{ibm2005blueprint,huebscher2008autonomic}.
\emph{Self-generation} (Section~\ref{sec:gen}) plans a new artifact: an LLM fills
a fixed template from indicator metadata and a data sample, probing live APIs
through a controlled fetch tool before writing code.
\emph{Self-deployment} (Section~\ref{sec:deploy}) is the execute step that needs
no operator: wrappers run as isolated processes the platform spawns, supervises,
checkpoints, and resumes from per-wrapper watermarks after restarts.
\emph{Self-healing} (Section~\ref{sec:heal}) is the full loop on a running
artifact: a monitor detects a crash, the failure is classified, the model
re-plans from the captured trace, and the repaired wrapper is redeployed.

This paper makes three contributions.
First, we give a source-agnostic observatory architecture and the contract that
makes it work: any source is reduced to a stream of normalized
$(\textrm{timestamp}, \textrm{value})$ points by a generated wrapper that the
platform owns end to end (Section~\ref{sec:arch}).
Second, we unify the three self-* properties as one autonomic manager over a
shared knowledge base, separating their MAPE-K roles and relating each to prior
work in autonomic computing, LLM code generation and repair, and wrapper
maintenance (Section~\ref{sec:selfstar}).
Third, we report measurements from a real \deploymentDays{}-day deployment
rather than a benchmark, including a pre-repair baseline (Section~\ref{sec:res-heal})
and a fault analysis that draws the manager's scope of control precisely
(Section~\ref{sec:res-faults}). \coderepo

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Smart city and tourism observatories}
Smart tourism research calls for destination-level data
infrastructures~\cite{gretzel2015smart,buhalis2013smart}, indicator schemes such
as ETIS define what such platforms should
measure~\cite{ec2016etis}, and implementation studies report uneven
adoption~\cite{modica2018assessment} and warehouse- or survey-based
dashboards~\cite{fuchs2014big}. Studies of smart destination programs repeatedly
identify data availability for sustained indicator monitoring as the
bottleneck~\cite{ivarsbaidal2021measuring,ivarsbaidal2023sustainable}. Reported
platforms rarely document the ingestion layer; we argue that source-agnostic
ingestion is what decides how many indicators an observatory can sustain, and we
document one in production. Although our deployment is a tourism observatory, the
ingestion pattern is domain-independent: it assumes only heterogeneous sources
and a common time-series target.

\subsection{Autonomic computing and self-* systems}
The autonomic computing vision frames self-managing components with closed
control loops as the answer to operational complexity~\cite{kephart2003vision},
organized as a monitor--analyze--plan--execute loop over a shared knowledge base
(MAPE-K)~\cite{ibm2005blueprint}, with self-configuration, self-healing, and
self-optimization as canonical properties surveyed
in~\cite{huebscher2008autonomic}. \sys{} instantiates the blueprint for data
wrappers and contributes a different actuator: where classical autonomic managers
plan over a fixed component's parameters, ours plans the component's source code
through an LLM, so self-configuration becomes code synthesis and self-healing
becomes code repair.

\subsection{LLMs for code generation, repair, and extraction}
LLMs generate working programs from natural
language~\cite{chen2021evaluating}, and program repair is increasingly
LLM-based~\cite{xia2023automated}. Feedback loops improve generated code:
Self-Refine~\cite{madaan2023selfrefine}, Reflexion~\cite{shinn2023reflexion},
and Self-Debugging~\cite{chen2024selfdebug}, though self-repair gains are not
uniform~\cite{olausson2024selfrepair}. On the data side, Evaporate showed that
generated extraction code amortizes inference cost across
documents~\cite{arora2023evaporate}, AutoScraper generates reusable
scrapers~\cite{huang2024autoscraper}, and prompted~\cite{narayan2022wrangle} and
instruction-tuned~\cite{zhang2024jellyfish} models perform classical data
preprocessing. In MAPE-K terms our planner reuses these techniques, but its
sensor is a production exit code rather than a unit test, and its plan rewrites
the whole managed artifact rather than a single faulting line.

\subsection{Wrapper induction, maintenance, and ETL automation}
Wrapper induction learned extraction rules from labeled
examples~\cite{kushmerick2000wrapper}; unsupervised systems inferred them from
page regularities~\cite{crescenzi2001roadrunner}; surveys review the
field~\cite{laender2002brief,ferrara2014web}. The maintenance problem,
detecting breakage and re-inducing the wrapper, was recognized
early~\cite{kushmerick2000verification,lerman2003wrapper}. ETL has been modeled
rather than synthesized~\cite{vassiliadis2002conceptual,vassiliadis2009survey},
with automation of schema matching~\cite{rahm2001survey},
curation~\cite{stonebraker2013tamer}, and
wrangling~\cite{furche2016wrangling}. \sys{} replaces both induction and
re-induction with LLM code generation, extending coverage beyond template pages
to arbitrary APIs and spreadsheets, and automates the narrow but recurrent ETL
slice indicator platforms need: single-series extraction into one normalized
time-series schema.

\section{A Source-Agnostic Observatory}
\label{sec:arch}

\Sys{} is a microservice platform: containerized user, knowledge-base,
data-collector, resource, and indicator services (Python, FastAPI) behind an
nginx gateway, plus a React dashboard. Two RabbitMQ brokers separate the control
plane from the data plane; the resource and indicator services each own a
MongoDB store. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{fig_arch}}
\caption{\Sys{} ingestion pipeline. The resource service generates, deploys, and
repairs wrappers; extracted points flow through two brokers to the indicator
store and the dashboard.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

The property that makes ingestion source-agnostic is a narrow contract. A
wrapper, whatever its source, publishes batches of points
$\{(x, y)\}$ with $x$ an ISO~8601 timestamp and $y$ a float; everything upstream
of that contract is generated per source, and everything downstream is fixed,
shared infrastructure. A domain user registers a source in a web wizard, either
uploading a CSV/XLSX file or configuring an API endpoint with its
authentication, and supplies indicator metadata (name, domain, unit,
periodicity, description). The resource service then owns the wrapper for its
whole lifecycle. The data collector validates each message against the contract,
caches the latest per wrapper, and forwards valid points to the control broker,
where the resource service maps wrapper identifiers to persistent resources and
the indicator service stores, merges, and serves the series. Source type enters
only as a parameter to one generation procedure, not as a separate codebase per
format, which is what lets the same machinery absorb a new kind of source.

\section{Self-* Wrappers as an Autonomic Manager}
\label{sec:selfstar}

We model each wrapper as the managed element of one autonomic manager. The
element is the generated artifact together with its runtime state: the wrapper's
source code, its execution watermarks, its retry counter, and its status. Over
this element the platform runs the MAPE-K loop~\cite{ibm2005blueprint}: it
\emph{monitors} process state, \emph{analyzes} terminations, \emph{plans} a new
or corrected artifact, and \emph{executes} the plan by deploying it, all against
a shared \emph{knowledge} base (Table~\ref{tab:mapek}). Figure~\ref{fig:selfstar}
shows the three self-* loops this decomposition produces. The actuator across all
of them is an LLM that writes and rewrites code, paired with a process supervisor
that deploys it.

\subsection{Shared knowledge}
\label{sec:knowledge}
The knowledge the loops read and write is what makes them one manager rather than
three mechanisms. It has a static part and a runtime part. The static part is the
fixed wrapper template, which is the contract of Section~\ref{sec:arch} expressed
as code, plus a list of fourteen hard rules distilled from failures seen during
development: never modify the historical-backfill driver, emit no synthetic data,
filter date ranges inclusively, keep timezones consistent, and parse non-English
dates such as localized month names. These bound what the planner may produce.
The runtime part is per-wrapper: two watermarks (the oldest and newest timestamps
ever sent) and a phase, a retry counter in $[0,3]$, a timestamped execution log,
and the persisted wrapper document holding metadata, source configuration,
current code, and status. Self-generation writes the initial code, self-deployment
reads and updates the watermarks, and self-healing reads the code and the log and
writes a new revision; the rule list, in particular, accumulates the lessons of
past failures and constrains every future plan.

\subsection{Self-generation: planning a new artifact}
\label{sec:gen}
Generation is constrained, not free-form. The model customizes a fixed wrapper
class whose variable methods are extraction (\texttt{fetch\_external\_data}) and,
for API sources, a windowed fetch and a polling interval; the backfill driver,
transport, batching, and run loop are fixed library code the prompt forbids it to
touch (Figure~\ref{fig:template}). Jinja2 assembles the prompt from the indicator
metadata, the source configuration, a short data sample (20 CSV lines, 15 rows
per workbook sheet with the sheet names, or one captured API response), the
target \texttt{\{x,y\}} schema, the template, per-source-type guidance, and the
rule list. For API sources the call also offers a sandboxed \texttt{fetch\_url}
tool that returns up to 2500 characters of a live response
(Figure~\ref{fig:gen}). The model may invoke it up to fifteen times, reading real
response nesting, field names, and honored date filters instead of guessing from
the URL; authentication is folded into request headers by the service from the
declared scheme, not handed to the model. A static gate runs before launch: the
file is parsed and scanned for unfilled placeholders, one failure triggers a
single lint-retry prompt, and a second marks the wrapper \texttt{error}. Nothing
is test-run before launch; runtime faults are left to self-healing.

\subsection{Self-deployment: execute without an operator}
\label{sec:deploy}
A generated wrapper reaches production with no human action. The resource service
launches each wrapper as its own operating-system process, handing it the wrapper
identifier and source configuration as arguments and the broker URL through the
environment. A semaphore caps how many run at once (100 by default), and a
per-process watchdog (3600\,s by default) bounds any single run. File wrappers run
once and exit; API wrappers backfill history in adaptive batches and then poll at
the cadence implied by the indicator periodicity (hourly becomes 3600\,s, daily
86\,400\,s), looping inside the process with no external scheduler. Points leave
in messages of at most 1000. A monitor samples each process's state every 30\,s.
Each wrapper also carries a low and a high watermark, the oldest and newest
timestamps it has sent; on relaunch it continues from the high mark instead of
re-pulling, so a restart neither duplicates nor drops points, with the resume
position passed in through the environment. One level up, a container auto-updater
rolls new service images into production without a manual deploy. Self-deployment
is the execute step that keeps the other two loops unattended: generation hands
the manager a process to supervise, and healing hands it a replacement to relaunch
and resume.

\subsection{Self-healing: the full loop on a live artifact}
\label{sec:heal}
Self-healing exercises every MAPE-K stage. The 30\,s poll reads each process's
exit code (\emph{monitor}). A nonzero exit, always code~1 in practice, is told
apart from an idle watchdog termination and from a generation-time provider
error, and only the first routes to repair (\emph{analyze}). The planner is an
LLM call given the whole crashed file, the captured stderr trace, and the last
twenty stdout lines, and asked to return a complete corrected file that keeps the
class structure and fixes that traceback (\emph{plan}); for API wrappers it may
re-probe the endpoint through the same \texttt{fetch\_url} tool, up to ten calls.
The reply clears the same static gate as initial generation, overwrites the file,
increments the retry counter, and the process is restarted and resumed from its
watermarks (\emph{execute}). At most three repairs are allowed per wrapper, after
which it is left \texttt{error} for an operator. Repairing a managed element
rather than patching a diff gives the loop two features: its sensor is a real
production exit code, possibly weeks after generation, so feedback reflects how
the source actually behaves; and because the planner re-emits the entire wrapper,
a fix can reshape the whole extraction path rather than edit the single offending
statement~\cite{chen2024selfdebug,olausson2024selfrepair}.

\subsection{Scope of control}
\label{sec:scope}
Source-agnosticism is then an emergent property of the manager rather than a
separate mechanism. The same knowledge and the same loops handle API, XLSX, and
CSV sources because source type is a parameter to one template, so a new kind of
source is absorbed by generating, deploying, and repairing its wrapper without a
human writing code. This also draws the manager's authority. Faults that lie in
the managed element, malformed extraction logic and source heterogeneity, are
inside its control and are removed by generation and repair; faults in the
environment around the element, exhausted model quota, wrong credentials, and
limits misconfigured for a source's cadence, are outside it. Section~\ref{sec:results}
quantifies both sides of that line.

\subsection{Self-healing effectiveness}
\label{sec:res-heal}
Of the generated wrappers, \nRetried{} (\pctRetried\%) raised an uncaught
exception at runtime and were handed to the repair loop, which issued
\nHealEvents{} redeployments; per-wrapper repair depth was \retryOnce{} at one
attempt, \retryTwice{} at two, and \retryThrice{} at three (by source type, 26
were XLSX, 14 API, and 3 CSV). What counts as recovery differs by yardstick, and
the yardsticks disagree. The crash recurred after the final repair for just one of
the 43, leaving 42 crash-free at the wrapper level. By final status, \nRecovered{}
of 43 (\pctRecovered\%) ended outside an error state. By output, 32 went on to
emit data after their last repair, 20{,}351 points, an eighth of the corpus. The
lone wrapper that used all three repairs never stabilized; it carries a non-error
final status only because its resource was later removed, so we do not read the
deepest-retry case as a win. Repairs were quick and hands-off: a median
\healLatencyMedian{}\,s from crash to redeploy (interquartile range 24.2--50.8\,s,
90th percentile \healLatencyPninety{}\,s, maximum \healLatencyMax{}\,s across the
\nHealEvents{} events), of which up to 30\,s is detection lag. The median failure
landed 16.8\,s into a run, inside the fetch-and-parse phase, and none struck while
a poller sat idle.

The window also supplies an unplanned control. No repair loop ran for the first
65 days (it enters the logs on 2026-03-19), and 36 crashes of this exit class over
that span were left to die, which we treat as the pre-automation baseline. Two
timeline effects temper the healing counts. Thirty-one of the 50 repair events
fall on one day, when a credential fix triggered a redeploy that relaunched every
wrapper at once, so the events are not independent. And because each relaunch
overwrites the stderr file, a triggering traceback survives only when the final
run also crashed; for wrappers that recovered we read the cause from the fixes
left in their code instead. Those fixes are diagnostic: a comment or guard naming
a repaired defect appears in 7 of the 43 crashed wrappers and in none of the 353
that never crashed, and timezone fixes in 4 of 43 against 1 of 353.
Table~\ref{tab:errors} adds the repair-depth breakdown to the fault analysis.

\subsection{Repair case studies}
\label{sec:res-cases}
Four wrappers illustrate the range. The first two are inferred from the repaired
code, the third quotes its original exception verbatim, and the fourth has a
preserved trace because it never healed.

\subsubsection{Naive-versus-aware datetime comparison (API, healed)}
An annual urban-waste API wrapper crashed 82\,s into a backfill when it compared
timezone-aware source dates against naive window bounds. The repair normalizes
both bounds to naive datetimes before comparing and logs instead of raising on an
empty fetch. Redeployed in 34.7\,s, the wrapper completed with 596 points.

\subsubsection{Duplicated date parameters (API, healed)}
An air-quality wrapper's configured endpoint URL already carried a date window,
and the generated code tacked on a second, so the server answered HTTP~400. The
repair strips every spelling of the start/end parameters from the configured URL
before applying its own window and absorbs residual 400s instead of crashing.
Redeployed in 51.3\,s, it was still streaming at the snapshot (395 points).

\subsubsection{Header token inside data rows (XLSX, healed)}
A cultural-heritage spreadsheet repeated its Portuguese year header
(\texttt{"Ano"}) inside the data, and the wrapper crashed on
\texttt{int("Ano")}; the repaired file quotes the exception verbatim, so the
cause is certain. The repair replaces raw integer casts with coercing conversion
and drops non-numeric rows, and three neighbouring wrappers picked up the
identical fix the same night. Redeployed in 61.6\,s, it completed.

\subsubsection{Absent second column (CSV, not healed)}
A search-interest export parsed into a single column, so no
$(\textrm{time}, \textrm{value})$ pair existed. Three repairs (27.0, 33.0, and
51.8\,s) added delimiter sniffing and bad-line skipping but could not invent a
column, and the loop gave up. A sibling wrapper on a two-column variant of the
same export healed on its first attempt. The manager repairs code; it cannot
manufacture data that the source does not contain.

\subsection{Faults inside and outside the manager}
\label{sec:res-faults}

Table~\ref{tab:errors} breaks the \nErrorW{} error-status wrappers down by root
cause, and almost none point to faulty generated code. A first group of 14 never
produced code at all: 9 hit the model's daily free-tier quota, one a per-request

A second and larger group, 54 wrappers, are API pollers the 3600\,s watchdog
killed between polling cycles, 53 of them without leaving a traceback; the
watchdog is simply tighter than these sources' daily or monthly cadence, a
configuration mismatch rather than a defect, and 48 had already shipped
52{,}372 points before the cut-off. One wrapper crashed and then could not be
repaired because the repair call itself hit the quota wall; this is the single
error-status wrapper that reflects generated code whose repair never deployed.
The division is the scope of control of Section~\ref{sec:scope} in numbers:
malformed extraction code and format quirks leave the operator's queue, while
exhausted quota, wrong credentials, and limits set for the wrong cadence stay
outside the managed element and stay manual.

\subsection{Pipeline integrity and throughput}
\label{sec:res-pipeline}
Points attenuate predictably along the pipeline: \nDataPoints{} sent by wrappers
become 148{,}131 raw and 106{,}442 consolidated points in the resource store, and
100{,}550 points in the indicator store that merge into series totaling 84{,}431
points. The attrition reflects deleted resources (198 of \nWrappers{}),
regeneration that clears prior data, points arriving for resources with no linked
indicator, and timestamp-level deduplication at merge time. Integrity held where
it was checked: the data collector logged no schema rejections over 30 days and
only 18 duplicate-timestamp warnings, which it resolved by averaging; in an
8.3-hour control-plane window the resource service published 153 messages and the
indicator service consumed exactly 153, with no loss across the broker; and the
front-door gateway served 55{,}086 requests over 20 days (about 2{,}747 per day,
80.3\% returning HTTP~200). Resumption worked in production: a container
recreation a few hours before the snapshot forced all 16 then-running wrappers to
restart, 15 resuming from their stored watermarks while the one without a
persisted lower mark replayed its history in full.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Reading the deployment through the autonomic manager explains both what worked
and where it stops. The manager keeps source-specific code out of human hands by
bounding generation with a fixed contract, grounding the plan in observed rather
than assumed source behavior, and triggering repair on a real production trace.
Its limits are exactly the faults outside the managed element: the only error
that reflects undeployed code repair sat behind a quota wall, and the only crash
the loop could not fix involved data that did not exist.

Several limitations qualify the measurements. They are observational, from one
deployment, one model family, and one region, with no contemporaneous control
arm; the pre-loop period differs in source mix and load, and the crash events are
clustered rather than independent. We cannot compare against hand-written
wrappers on the same sources, which were never built. Recovery counts wrappers
restored to a working state, not the semantic correctness of every extracted
value, since validation covers schema rather than ground truth, and for healed
wrappers the trigger is inferred from repair artifacts because the relaunch
overwrites the trace. The platform pins the model through a floating version
alias, so the underlying model may have changed during the window; generation
latency includes queueing and is an upper bound on model time; the memory
footprint is an idle measurement; and watermark-based coverage spans are
computable for only the 11 wrappers whose lower mark was persisted.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A smart city observatory becomes source-agnostic when its wrappers manage
themselves. Casting each wrapper as the managed element of one MAPE-K manager
unifies three properties that are usually treated separately: the manager plans
a wrapper from metadata and a sample, executes it as a supervised process that
resumes from checkpoints, and re-plans it from production traces when it breaks,
all over a shared knowledge base of a fixed contract and accumulated repair
rules. In \deploymentDays{} days the manager sustained \nWrappers{} wrappers and
\nDataPoints{} points; of \nRetried{} that crashed, all but one were left
crash-free by at most three repairs, at a median of \healLatencyMedian{}\,s each
and with no operator involvement. The faults it could not absorb were
environmental, which points the way forward: semantic validation of extracted
values, and an autonomic treatment of quota and credential faults as managed
state in their own right rather than as work left on the operator's desk.
